Personal practice

The Four
Immeasurables

A daily practice of an open heart.

20 minutes   ·   Guided practice   ·   Quiet intervals

Settle somewhere comfortable. Let the words become your own, and give yourself time in the quiet.

Read the transcript

Take a moment to feel the support beneath you. Find a position that feels steady and comfortable. Your eyes can rest softly open, or close if that feels right. Let your hands settle. Allow your shoulders to soften, and give your jaw a little room. For these next few minutes, you can set aside whatever can wait.

Notice one breath arriving, and one breath leaving. Let your breathing find its own rhythm. You may feel it at your nostrils, in your chest, or in your belly. Choose somewhere simple to rest your attention. When your mind wanders, notice where it went, and gently come back. Each return is part of the practice.

Whatever kind of day you've had, you can begin here. Let tiredness be tiredness, and restlessness be restlessness. There's room for what you're bringing. We will spend this time cultivating loving-kindness, compassion, joy in the happiness of others, and equanimity. There will be quiet intervals along the way. Let them give you time to practice.

We begin with refuge in the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Sangha. We turn toward the Buddha as our teacher, the Dharma as the path, and the noble Sangha as our guides. These words are offered in a plain-English adaptation. You can repeat them inwardly, or listen and connect with their meaning.

Until awakening, I place my trust in the Buddha, the Dharma, and the noble Sangha. May their example and guidance help me understand suffering and walk the path of freedom. I offer this practice for the welfare of every being. May I awaken fully, so that I can help others become free.

Let that intention settle. You are included in this wish for freedom, along with everyone you know and the countless beings you will never meet. For now, allow the intention to be sincere, even if it feels small. We can begin with the willingness to care.

Bring to mind someone for whom goodwill comes easily. It could be a friend, a teacher, or an animal you care for. Let a face or a simple sense of their presence be enough. Consider their wish to live with ease, to feel safe, and to find a happiness they can rely on. Offer this wish gently. May you be well. May you find lasting happiness, and the conditions that support it.

Now include yourself in that same kindness. Feel the body sitting here, breathing, doing its best to meet this life. May I be well. May I learn what brings lasting happiness. May I have the patience and courage to nurture those conditions. If these words feel unfamiliar, let them be an intention you can grow into.

Bring to mind someone you see without knowing much about them. Perhaps someone you pass in a store or on a walk. Their life matters to them as yours matters to you. Offer them the same goodwill. Then allow the wish to widen beyond any single person. May every being find lasting happiness. May the causes of that happiness grow. Stay with this wish for a few breaths.

Let kindness turn gently toward suffering. Bring to mind a difficulty you can hold without becoming overwhelmed. It may be your own difficulty, or something someone else is facing. You don't need to replay the whole story. Recognize that this is hard, and that relief matters. Let your breathing remain easy as you offer a wish for freedom.

May this suffering ease. May its causes be understood and released. May there be support, wisdom, and a path forward. Allow these wishes to include you. If the contemplation becomes too much, feel your feet or the support beneath you. You can open your eyes and let the image go. Begin again with something gentler whenever you are ready.

Now let compassion reach further. There are beings experiencing pain, fear, loneliness, and uncertainty. You can wish them freedom without carrying every story. May those who suffer find relief. May harmful actions cease, and may the confusion that feeds them give way to understanding. May all beings become free of suffering and its causes. Rest with the wish to help, in whatever wise and practical ways become possible.

Let your attention turn toward something good in another person's life. Choose a moment you can appreciate. Perhaps someone received help, learned something, recovered their confidence, or simply enjoyed a peaceful afternoon. Picture the ease on their face. Allow yourself to welcome that happiness. I'm glad this goodness is present in your life. May it continue to grow.

If comparison or envy appears, notice it with patience. You can feel a difficult response and still choose to wish someone well. Return to the person and the good you are recognizing. Their happiness needn't be measured against your own. May you enjoy what is wholesome and good. May the kindness and understanding in your life deepen.

You can also appreciate goodness in yourself. Recall a small act of patience, generosity, or restraint. Let yourself value it, without needing to make it larger. Then widen your appreciation to goodness wherever it appears. Somewhere, someone is being helped. Somewhere, a person is choosing kindness. May that goodness flourish. May all beings enjoy a happiness that is free from suffering. Take a little time to rejoice.

Bring to mind someone close to you, someone you barely know, and someone you find mildly difficult. Choose a difficulty you can meet with steadiness today. Notice how your feelings change as your attention moves among them. Underneath those different feelings, each of these people wants happiness. Each wishes to avoid suffering. Let that shared wish become clear.

Offer each person the same intention. May you find happiness and freedom from suffering. You can wish someone well while keeping clear boundaries. Care can remain present when you disagree, or when distance is needed. Gently loosen the habit of deciding whose well-being counts by how you happen to feel about them.

Let the circle open to all beings, near and far. May my care become less partial. May attachment and aversion loosen their hold. May I meet others with steadiness, wisdom, and an open heart. Many conditions shape a life, and outcomes are not entirely in our hands. Stay connected to the care you can offer, and allow room for what you cannot control.

Now let the phrases and images settle. Feel the body breathing. Allow sounds and sensations to come and go. There's no need to decide how well the practice has gone. We will rest quietly for a while. If your attention drifts, gently return to the feeling of sitting here.

Let the next breath be simple. If kindness is present, let it be present. If you feel very little, you can rest with that too. Give yourself these remaining moments without asking for a different experience.

We close by dedicating whatever goodness has arisen. Even a moment of sincere care can be included. You can offer this dedication inwardly. May the goodness of this practice support the awakening of all beings. May it help us understand suffering, abandon its causes, and cultivate wisdom and compassion. May I continue on this path, and become more able to benefit others.

Before moving on, let one simple intention come to mind. Perhaps you will listen with more patience, speak with care, welcome someone else's good fortune, or pause before reacting. Choose something ordinary and possible. Let it be a way for this practice to continue in your life.

Feel the support beneath you again. Notice the room around you. Let your eyes open, or lift your gaze, when you are ready. Move your hands and feet gently. Take your time returning. May this care accompany whatever comes next. May all beings be free.

About the prayers

The refuge, aspirations, and closing dedication are plain-English adaptations for this personal practice. They follow a Tibetan Mahayana orientation and are not a verbatim lineage liturgy.

The practice begins with refuge and the wish to awaken for the benefit of all beings. It closes by dedicating whatever goodness arose toward that same freedom.

Traditional grounding: Patrul Rinpoche on refuge and the awakening intention, and Khenpo Shenga on beginning and dedication.
